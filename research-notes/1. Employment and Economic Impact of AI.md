# Employment & Economic Impact of AI

### Productivity Growth and Economic Value
- Generative AI has huge potential to boost productivity. McKinsey estimates that generative AI could create US$ 2.6–4.4 trillion in annual value across many use cases. 

- A Cognizant–Oxford Economics study predicts that generative AI could increase U.S. GDP by $477 billion to $1 trillion annually over 10 years, depending on adoption.

### Job Disruption and Displacement

- About 6–7% of U.S. employment could be displaced if AI is widely adopted. 

- But the impact is likely to be transitional: Goldman Sachs argues many of the displacements will be temporary, with new jobs emerging. 

- The IMF suggests that in advanced (developed) economies, ~60% of jobs may be “exposed” to AI in some way; but “exposed” doesn’t necessarily mean full replacement — many of these jobs could benefit (complemented) rather than be destroyed.

### Income Effects and Inequality

- A working paper from the IMF (via their economic research) estimates that, on average, workers could lose ~4% of their earnings due to AI: more routine jobs are more negatively impacted, while high-skill jobs are less so. 
IMF

- The OECD Employment Outlook (2023) notes that AI could lead to worsening job quality and inequality: tasks that are automated may squeeze some workers into simpler, lower-paid tasks, while workers who can complement AI may see wage premiums. 
OECD

- According to the IZA (Institute of Labor Economics), AI tends to increase demand for “complementary” skills (e.g., digital literacy, teamwork) and creates wage premiums for those — but could worsen income inequality for lower-skilled or more routine roles. 
IZA World of Labor

- There’s research suggesting that “augmentation AI” (AI that helps people work better) raises wages in high-skilled occupations, whereas “automation AI” (AI that replaces work) reduces wages and jobs in lower-skilled ones. 
arXiv

### Labor Market Dynamics & Skills

- A study looking at LLMs (large language models) showed that ~80% of U.S. workers might have at least 10% of their tasks affected by LLMs; ~19% of workers may have half or more of their tasks affected. 
arXiv

- Another (very recent) academic paper argues that complement effects (AI boosting demand for certain human skills) might be 50% larger than substitution effects (AI replacing people), meaning net job creation in some skill areas. 
arXiv

- But, as AI adoption grows, workers will need reskilling and retraining in AI-complementary skills (digital literacy, adaptability, etc.) to avoid falling behind.
---
Not all data shows big job losses yet. For example, Bank of America research suggests AI’s impact on employment has been negligible so far, and that AI is currently more of a capital-expenditure (capex) story than a job-destroyer.
